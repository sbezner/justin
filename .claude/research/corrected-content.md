## The correction that drives this rewrite

The previous version's central claim — that Bastrop County voted for John C. Frémont in
1856 — **is false**. Frémont was not on the ballot in Texas and received no votes in the
state; the 1856 Texas contest was Buchanan vs. Fillmore. The Handbook of Texas entry for
Bastrop County contains this error, and the site inherited it.

Consequence: the hero's "voted for the party's *first* presidential nominee in 1856 …
and then came all the way back" is gone. So is the circular "there and back" arc.

**The replacement arc — three parties, one name:**

> The Republican Party has carried Bastrop County three separate times, and it was a
> different party each time. In the 1870s and 1880s it was the party of freedmen at
> Cedar Creek — and it was destroyed, first by violence and then by law. In 1972 and
> 1984 it was a landslide-year visitor that did not stay. Since 2000 it has been the
> county's governing majority. One name, three parties, a hundred and sixty years.

This framing must appear on the page. A standing note that the Republican Party of 1856,
1872 and 2024 are different coalitions sharing a name — and that this page tracks a name
across time — belongs near the top.

## Chapter I — The Little Colony (1827–1837)

Unchanged from the current page, with one flag: these colonization facts were **not
independently verified**. Keep them, and keep them modest.

- Stephen F. Austin obtained a grant for a "Little Colony" from the Mexican government
  in 1827. By 1830 the town of Bastrop had been founded; settlers from Austin's lower
  colonies were clearing farms across the southern part of the county.
- A second grant followed in 1831. Together the two became the Mina Municipality.
- March 17, 1836: Mina became an official part of Texas. December 18, 1837: the town of
  Bastrop formally born.
- Named for Felipe Enrique Neri, the "Baron de Bastrop."

## Chapter II — The County That Voted No (1848–1861)

**This chapter is rebuilt.** The anchor is no longer Frémont. It is an actual county vote
count.

Body:

- Bastrop County went for the **Whigs in 1848 and 1852**, and for
  **John Bell of the Constitutional Union Party in 1860** — the ticket of men trying to
  hold the country together without joining either sectional camp. A pattern, not an
  accident: three elections running, the county declined the Southern Democratic
  consensus.
- **February 23, 1861 — the secession referendum. Bastrop County voted against
  secession, 352 to 335.** Seventeen votes. It was one of only about **18 of the 122
  counties reporting** to reject secession, in a statewide result of roughly **76% in
  favor**.
- And then: the county armed and equipped Confederate companies anyway, and provided for
  soldiers' families.

The closing beat — this is the honest complication, keep it:

> A county can vote one way and march another. Bastrop's anti-secession majority was a
> Unionist majority, not a Republican one — there was no Republican Party on the ballot
> in Texas to be a majority for.

**Explicit correction callout on the page** (not buried in sources):

> **A note on 1856.** An earlier version of this page repeated the Handbook of Texas's
> statement that most Bastrop County voters chose John C. Frémont, the first Republican
> presidential nominee, in 1856. That cannot be right: Frémont was not on the ballot in
> Texas and received no votes in the state. The 1856 race here was Buchanan against
> Fillmore. The claim has been removed.

## Chapter III — The Party of Lincoln at Cedar Creek (1866–1890)

**Extended in both directions.** It must no longer end on the 1888 triumph.

Timeline entries, in order:

- **1866** — **Jeremiah J. Hamilton**, born enslaved in Tennessee in July 1838, brought
  to Texas as a slave in 1847, literate despite the law against it, becomes a spokesman
  for Black workers and founds a school for Black students in Bastrop.
- **1867** — Hamilton **sits on the board that registers Bastrop County voters**.
  Freedmen's Bureau agents and federal troops ran registration across Texas in 1867–68.
  *(The old page described this drive generically; it has a name and a local face.)*
- **1868–69** — **Morgan C. Hamilton** sits in the Constitutional Convention as a
  delegate associated with Bastrop County; **Julius Schuetze**, a German-Texan Unionist,
  sits for Fayette and Bastrop. The convention seated 90 delegates, 80 white and 10
  Black. *(Flag: Morgan Hamilton's Bastrop seat is **moderate confidence** — his life was
  centered on Austin. Phrase with care; do not overstate.)*
- **1869** — Black men vote in Texas statewide elections for the first time.
  **Cal (Calvin) Thompson**, born enslaved c. 1828 in Tennessee, buys land at
  **Cedar Creek** — at the suggestion of his former enslaver, Marshall Trigg of Hills
  Prairie — and builds it to **500 acres**. The local newspaper calls him "a Negro of
  much influence in the community."
- **1870–73** — Jeremiah J. Hamilton, elected Republican in 1869, serves in the
  **Texas House, Twelfth Legislature, February 9, 1870 – January 14, 1873**,
  representing Bastrop and Fayette counties.
- **1868–1900 (context)** — **fifty-two African American men served Texas either in the
  legislature or as delegates to a constitutional convention**; forty-one served in the
  legislature between 1868 and 1900. The Reconstruction-era cohort were Republicans.
  **Do not write "52 legislators during Reconstruction" — that was the old error.**
- **May 1888** — **Orange Wicks** elected justice of the peace and **Ike Wilson**
  elected constable for the Cedar Creek precinct. Wicks was the **first African American
  elected justice of the peace in Bastrop County**.
  **Spelling: WICKS, not Weeks.** ("Weeks" is a variant; Wicks is what TSHA uses.)
- **June 13, 1889 — the Cedar Creek shootout.** Tension around an assault case against
  Andy Litton. Justice **Wicks called the case**; a defendant refused to let Constable
  **Ike Wilson** serve papers. **Four men were killed — two white, two Black.**
  **Wicks and Wilson were arrested and charged with murder.** Afterward, a considerable
  number of African Americans were killed or forced into exile from Cedar Creek.
- **April 21, 1890** — **Cal Thompson is shot dead** by two white men on the
  Bastrop–Cedar Creek road, more than four and a half miles west of Bastrop. Governor
  Lawrence Sullivan Ross posted a **$250 reward**. Newspapers called him "a respectable
  and peaceable colored man."
- **1884 → 1896** — **Cedar Creek's population falls from 600 to 250.**
  *(The −58% is our arithmetic; say so or omit the percentage.)*

Closing beat for the chapter:

> Eighteen months separated the swearing-in from the shooting. The county's Republican
> electorate did not drift away or change its mind. It was destroyed — and the law
> arrived thirteen years later to formalize what the violence had already done.

## Chapter IV — The Long Century (1876–1968)

**Date range corrected.** The Handbook says "1874 through 1968," but 1874 is not a
presidential year. The presidential run is **1876–1968 — twenty-four elections**.

- Democratic presidential candidates carried Bastrop County in **virtually every**
  election from 1876 through 1968. **Keep the word "virtually."** The chart must not
  harden the hedge into an absolute.
- The single interruption: **1896, William McKinley, a plurality** — not a majority.
- **1872** — Horace Greeley carried most of the county's votes, as the fusion nominee of
  the Democratic Party and the Liberal Republicans. It was the first year county voters
  could vote for president after Reconstruction; Texas cast no electoral votes in 1864 or
  1868. Counted on the Democratic side, as the Handbook counts him.

**New material — the mechanism.** The old page treated the silence as apathy. Name the
cause:

- The violence came first (1889–90), a decade before any statute.
- **1902 — the poll tax** ($1.50–$1.75). A federal court later found its primary purpose
  "was the desire to disenfranchise the Negro and the poor white supporters of the
  Populist Party" (*United States v. Texas*, 252 F. Supp. 234, W.D. Tex. 1966).
- **1903 — the Terrell Election Law**, which let parties restrict primary participation
  and paved the way for the **white primary** — decisive in a one-party state, where the
  Democratic primary *was* the election.

Rewrite the "a Republican voter in 1930 could not remember…" line. In 1930 many of the
county's would-be Republican voters were not failing to remember a victory — **they were
barred from voting.** Name that.

## Chapter V — The False Dawn (1972–1996)

**This chapter is renamed and its meaning reversed.** It was called "The Turn." It was
not a turn. This is the single biggest factual improvement on the page.

- **1972 — Richard Nixon** took Bastrop County with a comfortable majority.
- **And then the county went back.** Democratic majorities in **1976** and **1988**.
- **1984 — Ronald Reagan** won it: the exception, in a 49-state landslide.
- **1992 and 1996 — Bill Clinton won the county, but only pluralities**, partly because
  **Ross Perot** drew off voters who might otherwise have gone Republican.

Framing:

> Nixon in 1972 looks like the beginning of the story only if you stop there. The county
> voted Democratic for president in 1976 and 1988, and gave Bill Clinton the most votes
> in 1992 and 1996. Twenty-eight years separate Nixon's win from the majority that
> actually stayed. What 1972 and 1984 have in common is not Bastrop County — it is that
> both were national landslides.

**Data honesty:** 1980 is **not** claimed. A vote total surfaced for it that could not be
re-verified, and the Handbook conspicuously omits the year. Leave 1980 unmarked and say
the record is incomplete. **Never interpolate 2008 or 2012.**

## Chapter VI — The Flip (2000–2004)

- **George W. Bush** carried Bastrop County with comfortable majorities in **2000** and
  **2004** — and this time it held. Every presidential election since has gone
  Republican.
- **2000 is the real hinge**, not 1972.
- By the early twenty-first century Republican candidates were competitive in races for
  local offices too. *(The old page put this in quotation marks as a Handbook quote. The

## Chapter VIII — Down the Ballot, and the Party Today

**New chapter.** The presidential flip and the local flip are different events, years
apart — that gap is the most interesting under-told fact about the modern county.

- **Clara Beckett (R)**, Commissioner Precinct 2, **elected 2002**, the longest-serving
  member of the commissioners court.
- **Ronnie McDonald (D)** was elected **county judge in 1998 at age 27** — the youngest
  county judge in Texas history and the first African American county judge in Bastrop
  County. He **did not seek re-election in 2012**, the year after the fire.
- **Paul Pape (R)** won the county judgeship in a **2012 special election**, re-elected
  2014 and 2018.
- **Maurice Cook (R)** won the **2016** Republican primary runoff for sheriff **by four
  votes**, then took the general with 58%.
- **Gregory Klaus (R)** won the 2022 runoff and took office January 2023.
- **Curtis Courtney** has chaired the county party since **June 2022** (a precinct chair
  from 2015).

The sentence that carries the chapter:

> The county judgeship passed from Democrat Ronnie McDonald to Republican Paul Pape in
> 2012, and the sheriff's office to Republican Maurice Cook in 2016 — eleven and fifteen
> years after the presidential vote had flipped.

**Do NOT claim** an all-Republican commissioners court, or a first-Republican-majority
year. That could not be established.

Party structure (unchanged, verified):
- Official local representative of the Republican Party of Texas.
- Governed by the **County Executive Committee** — the county chair plus precinct chairs,
  one elected per voting precinct.
- Public calendar, precinct directory and newsletter at bastropctygop.com; FEC committee

## The 2011 fire — include, but frame precisely

- **September 4, 2011.** Burned 55 days. The **most destructive wildfire in Texas
  history**: roughly **1,600–1,700 homes** destroyed, **two deaths**, about **$325M** in
  losses, **~95% of Bastrop State Park**, and **32,400 acres of the Lost Pines**. More
  than **4 million pines** replanted beginning January 2013.
- **459 homes rebuilt by 2013** — 46% of the 2011 stock and 75% of all rebuilding from
  2012–21. **747** destroyed homes belonged to families with no resources or inadequate
  insurance.

**Framing discipline — this matters.** There is **no source** for the claim that the fire
changed the county's partisan composition, and the rebuild data cuts against it (existing
residents rebuilding on their own lots). The county's transformation is far better
explained by Austin-driven in-migration, which **predates the fire** — the county roughly
doubled between 1990 and 2010. Present the fire as **civic rupture with one documented
political consequence** (McDonald's 2012 retirement, after which the judgeship went
Republican and stayed), **not** as a demographic cause.
