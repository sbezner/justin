Chapter One

The Little Colony

1827 — 1837

Before there was a party, there was a place: a bend of the Colorado River where the blackland prairie runs into a stand of loblolly pine that has no business being there. Everything that follows happens on this ground.

Settlement began after Stephen F. Austin obtained a grant for a "Little Colony" from the Mexican government in 1827. By 1830 the town of Bastrop had been founded and settlers from Austin's lower colonies were clearing farms across the southern part of the county. A second grant followed in 1831; together the two became the Mina Municipality, taking in nearly all of what is now Bastrop County.

On March 17, 1836 — in the middle of a revolution — Mina became an official part of Texas. The town of Bastrop was formally born on December 18, 1837. The name honors Felipe Enrique Neri, the "Baron de Bastrop," the Dutch businessman who negotiated with Mexican officials to make Austin's colonization possible.

Chapter Two

A County Votes for Frémont

1856 — 1860

In 1856, most voters in Bastrop County cast their ballots for John C. Frémont — the candidate of a brand-new party called the Republicans.

It is the fact that startles people who assume the county's politics arrived recently. The Republican Party ran its first presidential campaign in 1856. In a slaveholding county on the edge of the Texas frontier, a majority of Bastrop's voters backed it.

Four years later, on the eve of war, the county went for John Bell of the Constitutional Union Party — the ticket of men trying to hold the country together without joining either sectional camp. Twice in a row, Bastrop County declined to follow the Southern Democratic consensus.

Then the war came, and the ground shifted under everyone.

Chapter Three

The Party of Lincoln at Cedar Creek

1867 — 1888

Reconstruction gave Bastrop County its first mass Republican electorate — and it was Black.
1867–68

Registration

Freedmen's Bureau agents and federal troops ran voter registration drives across Texas.

1869

The first ballots

Black men voted in Texas statewide elections for the first time. The same year, Cal Thompson — freed from slavery and by then a leader among Black residents of Bastrop County — bought land in the Cedar Creek community.

1870s

Fifty-two legislators

Fifty-two African American men served in the Texas Legislature during Reconstruction. Every one of them was a Republican — in a state where secessionist Democrats had carried the Confederacy.

1870s–80s

And the backlash

Reconstruction in Bastrop County brought the same tensions felt across the South. Racial confrontations flared around the community of Cedar Creek.

May 1888

Weeks and Wilson

Black political strength at Cedar Creek grew through the 1870s and 1880s. In the elections of May 1888, Orange Weeks was elected justice of the peace and Ike Wilson constable for the Cedar Creek precinct.

Chapter Four

The Long Century

1874 — 1968

Then the door closed. From 1874 through 1968, Democratic presidential candidates carried Bastrop County in virtually every single election — a run of nearly a century.

There was exactly one interruption. In 1896, Republican William McKinley won a plurality of the county's votes. One year out of twenty-four.

It is worth sitting with the length of that. A Republican voter in Bastrop County in 1930 could not remember a Republican carrying the county, and neither could their parents.

Chapter Five

The Turn

1972

1972

Richard Nixon took Bastrop County with a comfortable majority — the first Republican to carry it outright since before anyone living could remember. After 1972, Republican candidates began to become genuinely competitive here.

Nothing about it was instant. The turn ran on the slow clock of rural Texas: county conventions in borrowed halls, precinct chairs recruited one at a time, down-ballot races that went uncontested for years before someone finally filed.

Chapter Six

Bush Country, and Down the Ballot

2000 — 2004

George W. Bush carried Bastrop County with comfortable majorities in both 2000 and 2004 — a Texas governor from just up the road, on a county map that had been trending his way for a generation.

The more consequential change was quieter. By the early twenty-first century, Republican candidates were fully competitive in races for local offices too: commissioners, judges, constables, the offices that actually run a county. A presidential preference had become a party organization.

Chapter Eight

The County Party Today

Now

What it is

The Bastrop County Republican Party is the official local representative of the Republican Party of Texas — the county-level body through which state party business actually happens.

How it governs itself

Its governing body is the County Executive Committee (CEC), made up of the county chair together with the precinct chairs — one elected per voting precinct. Power in a county party is precinct power.

Where to find it

The party keeps a public calendar of monthly meetings and events, a precinct-chair directory, and a newsletter at bastropctygop.com. It is a registered committee with the FEC (C00761148) and a member of the local chambers of commerce.

From Frémont in 1856 to a full county ballot in 2024 is a hundred and sixty-eight years, and the line between those two points is anything but straight. It runs through Reconstruction and its collapse, through a ninety-four-year drought, through Cedar Creek and Nixon and Bush, and out into a county adding people faster than almost anywhere in Texas.

The Lost Pines are still standing. The story isn't finished.
