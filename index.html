Bastrop County · Texas · An independent history

Red Clay&Lost Pines

The Republican Party has carried Bastrop County three times, and it was a different party each time — the freedmen's party at Cedar Creek, a landslide-year visitor, and the governing majority it is today.

What this page tracks. The Republican Party of 1856, of 1872 and of 2024 are different coalitions that share a name. This is a history of the name in one county, not of a single continuous organization.

Chapter One

The Little Colony

1827–1837

Before there was a party, there was a place.

Settlement began after Stephen F. Austin obtained a grant for a “Little Colony” from the Mexican government in 1827. By 1830 the town of Bastrop had been founded, and settlers from Austin's lower colonies were clearing farms across the southern part of the county. A second grant followed in 1831; together the two became the Mina Municipality.

On March 17, 1836 Mina became an official part of Texas. The town of Bastrop was formally born on December 18, 1837. The name honours Felipe Enrique Neri, the “Baron de Bastrop.”

The ground is a bend of the Colorado River where blackland prairie runs into a stand of loblolly pine that has no business being there — an isolated population a hundred miles west of the forest it belongs to. Everything that follows happens here.

Chapter Two

The County That Voted No

1848–1861

Three elections running, the county declined the Southern Democratic consensus. Then it voted against secession by seventeen votes.

Bastrop County voted against secession, 352 to 335.

Bastrop County went for the Whigs in 1848 and 1852, and for John Bell of the Constitutional Union Party in 1860 — the ticket of men trying to hold the country together without joining either sectional camp. A pattern, not an accident: three elections running, the county declined the Southern Democratic consensus.

On February 23, 1861 the secession referendum came. Bastrop County voted against secession, 352 to 335. Seventeen votes. It was one of only about 18 of the 122 counties reporting to reject secession, in a statewide result of roughly 76% in favour.

And then the county armed and equipped Confederate companies anyway, and provided for soldiers' families.

A county can vote one way and march another. Bastrop's anti-secession majority was a Unionist majority, not a Republican one — there was no Republican Party on the ballot in Texas to be a majority for.

Correction

A note on 1856

An earlier version of this page repeated the Handbook of Texas's statement that most Bastrop County voters chose John C. Frémont, the first Republican presidential nominee, in 1856. That cannot be right: Frémont was not on the ballot in Texas and received no votes in the state. The 1856 race here was Buchanan against Fillmore. The claim has been removed.

Chapter Three

The Party of Lincoln at Cedar Creek

1866–1890

Reconstruction gave Bastrop County its first mass Republican electorate — and it was Black.

BastropElginSmithvilleCedar Creek McDadeRed RockPaigeRosankyString Prairie

  1. 1866

    Jeremiah J. Hamilton

    Born enslaved in Tennessee in July 1838 and brought to Texas as a slave in 1847, Jeremiah J. Hamilton was literate despite the law against it. He becomes a spokesman for Black workers and founds a school for Black students in Bastrop.

  2. 1867

    Who gets to register

    Hamilton sits on the board that registers Bastrop County voters. Freedmen's Bureau agents and federal troops ran registration across Texas in 1867–68.

  3. 1868–69

    The Constitutional Convention

    Morgan C. Hamilton sits in the Constitutional Convention as a delegate associated with Bastrop County; Julius Schuetze, a German-Texan Unionist, sits for Fayette and Bastrop. The convention seated ninety delegates, eighty white and ten Black.

    Morgan Hamilton's Bastrop seat is held here with moderate confidence; his life was centred on Austin.

  4. 1869

    The first ballots, and five hundred acres

    Black men vote in Texas statewide elections for the first time. Cal (Calvin) Thompson, born enslaved about 1828 in Tennessee, buys land at Cedar Creek — at the suggestion of his former enslaver, Marshall Trigg of Hills Prairie — and builds it to 500 acres. The local newspaper calls him “a Negro of much influence in the community.”

  5. 1870–73

    The Twelfth Legislature

    Elected as a Republican in 1869, Jeremiah J. Hamilton serves in the Texas House, Twelfth Legislature, February 9, 1870 – January 14, 1873, representing Bastrop and Fayette counties.

  6. 1868–1900

    Fifty-two men

    Across this period fifty-two African American men served Texas either in the legislature or as delegates to a constitutional convention; forty-one served in the legislature between 1868 and 1900. The Reconstruction-era cohort were Republicans.

  7. May 1888

    Wicks and Wilson

    Orange Wicks is elected justice of the peace and Ike Wilson constable for the Cedar Creek precinct. Wicks was the first African American elected justice of the peace in Bastrop County.

  8. June 13, 1889

    The Cedar Creek shootout

    Tension around an assault case against Andy Litton. Justice Wicks called the case; a defendant refused to let Constable Ike Wilson serve papers. Four men were killed — two white, two Black. Wicks and Wilson were arrested and charged with murder. Afterward, a considerable number of African Americans were killed or forced into exile from Cedar Creek.

  9. April 21, 1890

    Cal Thompson is killed

    Thompson is shot dead by two white men on the Bastrop–Cedar Creek road, more than four and a half miles west of Bastrop. Governor Lawrence Sullivan Ross posted a $250 reward. Newspapers called him “a respectable and peaceable colored man.”

  10. 1884 → 1896

    Cedar Creek empties

    The community's population falls from 600 to 250 — a decline of 58%. derived

    The two counts are the source's. The percentage is our arithmetic.

Eighteen months separated the swearing-in from the shooting. The county's Republican electorate did not drift away or change its mind. It was destroyed — and the law arrived thirteen years later to formalize what the violence had already done.

Chapter Four

The Long Century

1876–1968

Twenty-four elections. One interruption. And a set of laws that made it so.

Then the door closed. From 1876 through 1968 — twenty-four presidential elections — Democratic candidates carried Bastrop County in virtually every one.

There was a single interruption. In 1896, Republican William McKinley won a plurality of the county's votes. Not a majority.

Four years earlier the county had a different kind of year. In 1872 Horace Greeley carried most of the county's votes as the fusion nominee of the Democratic Party and the Liberal Republicans. It was the first year county voters could vote for president after Reconstruction; Texas cast no electoral votes in 1864 or 1868. Greeley is counted here on the Democratic side, as the Handbook counts him.

It is worth naming what produced the silence, because it was not apathy. The violence came first — 1889 and 1890, a decade before any statute. Then the statutes arrived.

In 1902 Texas adopted a poll tax of $1.50 to $1.75. A federal court later found that its primary purpose was the desire to disenfranchise the Negro and the poor white supporters of the Populist Party. In 1903 the Terrell Election Law let parties restrict primary participation and paved the way for the white primary — decisive in a one-party state, where the Democratic primary was the election.

So: in 1930, many of the county's would-be Republican voters were not failing to remember a Republican victory. They were barred from voting.

Chapter Five

The False Dawn

1972–1996

Nixon in 1972 looks like the beginning of the story only if you stop there.

1972

Richard Nixon took Bastrop County with a comfortable majority.

And then the county went back. Democratic majorities in 1976 and 1988. In 1984 Ronald Reagan won it — the exception, in a forty-nine-state landslide. In 1992 and 1996 Bill Clinton won the county, but only pluralities, partly because Ross Perot drew off voters who might otherwise have gone Republican.

Twenty-eight years separate Nixon's win from the majority that actually stayed. What 1972 and 1984 have in common is not Bastrop County — it is that both were national landslides.

Chapter Six

The Flip

2000–2004

2000 is the real hinge, not 1972.

George W. Bush carried Bastrop County with comfortable majorities in 2000 and 2004 — and this time it held. Every presidential election since has gone Republican.

That is the difference between 1972 and 2000, and it is the whole difference. 2000 is the real hinge.

By the early twenty-first century Republican candidates were competitive in races for local offices too — commissioners, judges, constables, the offices that actually run a county. A presidential preference was becoming a party organization. It would take another decade to finish.

The presidential flip and the local flip are different events — eleven and fifteen years apart. Chapter Eight.

Interlude

The Fire

September 4, 2011

The most destructive wildfire in Texas history burned through the Lost Pines for fifty-five days.

Thirty-two thousand four hundred acres of the Lost Pines.

The Bastrop County Complex Fire began on September 4, 2011 and burned fifty-five days. It destroyed roughly 1,600 to 1,700 homes, killed two people, caused about $325 million in losses, and took about 95% of Bastrop State Park and 32,400 acres of the Lost Pines. More than four million pines were replanted beginning in January 2013.

By 2013, 459 homes had been rebuilt — 46% of the 2011 stock, and 75% of all rebuilding done between 2012 and 2021. 747 of the destroyed homes had belonged to families with no resources or inadequate insurance.

A caution about cause

The fire is not the explanation

There is no source for the claim that the fire changed the county's partisan composition, and the rebuilding data cuts against it — the people who rebuilt were largely existing residents, on their own lots. The county's transformation is far better explained by Austin-driven in-migration, which predates the fire: the county roughly doubled between 1990 and 2010.

The fire belongs on this page as a civic rupture with one documented political consequence — County Judge Ronnie McDonald did not seek re-election in 2012, and the judgeship went Republican and stayed. It does not belong here as a demographic cause.

Chapter Seven

The Margin, and the Ground It Stands On

2020–2024

Two elections with verified county returns, and a county adding people faster than almost anywhere in Texas.

These are the only presidential vote totals on this page. Everything before them is direction — who carried the county — because that is what the record supports.

In 2020, 17 of 22 voting precincts went majority Republican; five went majority Democratic.

Reported Election Atlas, 2020 Single source; moderate-to-high confidence.

In the 2024 general election, Republican candidates won every race on the Bastrop County ballot.

Almost none of that growth is babies; nearly all of it is arrivals. Every county party in Central Texas is now organizing a county that is partly made of strangers — which is either the opportunity or the threat, depending on who you ask.

Chapter Eight

Down the Ballot, and the Party Today

Now

The presidential flip and the local flip are different events, years apart.

The county judgeship passed from Democrat Ronnie McDonald to Republican Paul Pape in 2012, and the sheriff's office to Republican Maurice Cook in 2016 — eleven and fifteen years after the presidential vote had flipped.

Not claimed

What this page does not assert

This page does not claim an all-Republican commissioners court, and it does not name a year in which a Republican majority was first achieved on it. Neither could be established from the record available here.

The Republican Party has carried Bastrop County three separate times, and it was a different party each time. In the 1870s and 1880s it was the party of freedmen at Cedar Creek — and it was destroyed, first by violence and then by law. In 1972 and 1984 it was a landslide-year visitor that did not stay. Since 2000 it has been the county's governing majority.

One name, three parties, a hundred and sixty years.

The Lost Pines are still standing. The story isn't finished.

Widely-syndicated tables that appear to give Bastrop County results for these years were found to reproduce Texas statewide figures instead, and were rejected. 2016 is likewise not documented on this page.
